### The problem

Classical noise suppression assumes the noise is **stationary** — that its spectrum barely changes from
one frame to the next. Spectral subtraction, Wiener filtering and LMS-based active noise control all
lean on that assumption, and they earn their keep on engine hum, rotor wash and road roar.

A gunshot breaks the assumption in a single frame. So does a door slam, a rotor blade passing the mic,
a siren sweeping through the speech band. The estimator is still describing the noise from 30 ms ago,
so it either fails to remove the event or, worse, removes the speech with it: pumping, musical noise
and clipped consonants. In the field that is the difference between a readable transmission and
a repeated one.

### The approach

Three stages, each doing only what it is good at.

- **DSP where DSP wins.** A reference microphone hears mostly the environment. Beamforming and an NLMS
  adaptive filter use it to subtract steady noise from the primary microphone, every 10 ms frame, for
  almost no compute.
- **A small network where DSP fails.** An RNNoise-style recurrent network predicts a gain per frequency
  band. It is only invoked when the prefiltered SNR is low or the residual looks impulsive, so the
  average cost stays inside the MCU's budget.
- **A post-filter for polish.** A comb filter locked to the speaker's pitch harmonics clears the
  residual noise sitting between them.

The whole chain runs on one microcontroller with no cloud, no phone and no companion DSP. What leaves
the device is speech, on a wireless link with a 25 ms budget end to end.
